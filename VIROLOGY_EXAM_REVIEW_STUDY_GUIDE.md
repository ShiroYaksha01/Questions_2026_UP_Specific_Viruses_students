## Lecture 1: Human Immunodeficiency Virus
**Total Questions:** 20 | **Focus Area:** High-Yield Medical & Clinical Virology

### Q1. HIV belongs to which family and genus?

- [ ] A) Picornaviridae – Enterovirus
- [x] **B) Retroviridae – Lentivirus** *(Correct Answer)*
- [ ] C) Flaviviridae – Hepacivirus
- [ ] D) Hepeviridae – Orthohepevirus

> **Correct Answer:** **B) Retroviridae – Lentivirus**
>
> **Explanation & Rationale:** HIV (Human Immunodeficiency Virus) belongs to the family Retroviridae and the genus Lentivirus ('lenti-' meaning slow, referring to the chronic, slow course of disease).
>
> **High-Yield Takeaway:** `HIV = Retroviridae, Genus Lentivirus (enveloped, ssRNA-RT).`

### Q2. The first isolation of a T-lymphotropic retrovirus from a patient at risk for AIDS occurred in:

- [ ] A) 1975
- [ ] B) 1981
- [x] **C) 1983** *(Correct Answer)*
- [ ] D) 1990

> **Correct Answer:** **C) 1983**
>
> **Explanation & Rationale:** Luc Montagnier, Françoise Barré-Sinoussi, and colleagues at the Institut Pasteur first isolated lymphadenopathy-associated virus (LAV, later named HIV-1) from a patient at risk for AIDS in 1983 (awarded the 2008 Nobel Prize in Medicine).
>
> **High-Yield Takeaway:** `1983: First isolation of HIV at the Pasteur Institute.`

### Q3. Among HIV-1 groups, the pandemic lineage is group M, which stands for:

- [ ] A) "Middle"
- [x] **B) "Main"** *(Correct Answer)*
- [ ] C) "Modified"
- [ ] D) "Minor"

> **Correct Answer:** **B) "Main"**
>
> **Explanation & Rationale:** HIV-1 is categorized into groups M, N, O, and P. Group M represents the 'Main' (or Major) pandemic group, accounting for over 95% of all HIV-1 infections worldwide.
>
> **High-Yield Takeaway:** `HIV-1 Group M = 'Main' (global pandemic lineage).`

### Q5. Which antiretroviral classes is HIV-2 intrinsically resistant to?

- [ ] A) NRTIs and PIs
- [x] **B) NNRTIs and T20 (fusion inhibitor)** *(Correct Answer)*
- [ ] C) INSTIs and CCR5 antagonists
- [ ] D) PIs and entry inhibitors

> **Correct Answer:** **B) NNRTIs and T20 (fusion inhibitor)**
>
> **Explanation & Rationale:** HIV-2 has natural, intrinsic resistance to all Non-Nucleoside Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitors (NNRTIs, e.g., efavirenz, nevirapine) and the fusion inhibitor Enfuvirtide (T20). HIV-2 must be treated with NRTI- and INSTI- or boosted PI-based regimens.
>
> **High-Yield Takeaway:** `HIV-2 intrinsic resistance: NNRTIs and T20 fusion inhibitor.`

### Q6. Which recombinant form is responsible for more than 90% of HIV-1 infections in Cambodia?

- [x] **A) CRF01_AE** *(Correct Answer)*
- [ ] B) CRF02_AG
- [ ] C) CRF01_AB
- [ ] D) CRF11_cpx

> **Correct Answer:** **A) CRF01_AE**
>
> **Explanation & Rationale:** In Southeast Asia, particularly Cambodia and Thailand, the circulating recombinant form CRF01_AE is predominant, accounting for >90% of all HIV-1 infections.
>
> **High-Yield Takeaway:** `CRF01_AE causes >90% of HIV infections in Cambodia.`

### Q7. In the HIV virion (100–120 nm), which protein forms the capsid?

- [ ] A) gp120 envelope
- [ ] B) p17 matrix
- [x] **C) p24** *(Correct Answer)*
- [ ] D) gp41 envelope

> **Correct Answer:** **C) p24**
>
> **Explanation & Rationale:** The HIV viral capsid (core) is conical and formed by the p24 protein (encoded by the gag gene). p24 is the major antigen measured in 4th-generation ELISA screening tests.
>
> **High-Yield Takeaway:** `Capsid protein = p24 (encoded by gag gene; key serological marker).`

### Q8. Which viral gene encodes the enzymes reverse transcriptase, integrase, and protease?

- [ ] A) gag
- [x] **B) pol** *(Correct Answer)*
- [ ] C) env
- [ ] D) nef

> **Correct Answer:** **B) pol**
>
> **Explanation & Rationale:** The pol (polymerase) gene encodes all essential viral enzymes: Reverse Transcriptase (RT), Integrase (IN), and Protease (PR). gag encodes matrix (p17), capsid (p24), and nucleocapsid (p7); env encodes envelope glycoproteins (gp120 and gp41).
>
> **High-Yield Takeaway:** `pol gene = Reverse Transcriptase, Integrase, and Protease.`

### Q9. Dolutegravir, raltegravir, and elvitegravir belong to which class of antiretrovirals, and which viral enzyme do they target?

- [ ] A) NNRTIs – reverse transcriptase
- [ ] B) PIs – protease
- [x] **C) INSTIs – integrase** *(Correct Answer)*
- [ ] D) NRTIs – reverse transcriptase

> **Correct Answer:** **C) INSTIs – integrase**
>
> **Explanation & Rationale:** Dolutegravir (DTG), raltegravir (RAL), and elvitegravir (EVG) are Integrase Strand Transfer Inhibitors (INSTIs) that selectively inhibit the HIV integrase enzyme, blocking integration of proviral DNA into host chromosomes.
>
> **High-Yield Takeaway:** `DTG, RAL, EVG = INSTIs targeting viral integrase.`

### Q10. The CCR5 antagonist maraviroc blocks HIV at which step of the replication cycle?

- [x] **A) Attachment of gp120 to CD4 and co-receptor** *(Correct Answer)*
- [ ] B) gp41-mediated membrane fusion
- [ ] C) Reverse transcription
- [ ] D) Protease-mediated maturation

> **Correct Answer:** **A) Attachment of gp120 to CD4 and co-receptor**
>
> **Explanation & Rationale:** Maraviroc is a chemokine receptor antagonist that binds selectively to host CCR5 co-receptors on CD4+ cells, preventing the interaction between viral gp120 and CCR5, thereby blocking viral attachment/entry.
>
> **High-Yield Takeaway:** `Maraviroc = CCR5 co-receptor antagonist (blocks viral entry).`

### Q11. Which exposure route carries the highest risk of HIV transmission?

- [ ] A) Receptive anal intercourse
- [ ] B) Mother-to-child transmission
- [x] **C) Blood transfusion** *(Correct Answer)*
- [ ] D) Percutaneous needle-stick

> **Correct Answer:** **C) Blood transfusion**
>
> **Explanation & Rationale:** Blood transfusion with contaminated blood carries the highest transmission probability per single exposure event (estimated at 90–92% or ~9,250 per 10,000 exposures), compared to receptive anal intercourse (~1.4%) or needle sharing (~0.6%).
>
> **High-Yield Takeaway:** `Highest per-exposure transmission risk: Blood transfusion (~92%).`

### Q15. A 4th-generation ELISA differs from earlier serological tests because it simultaneously detects:

- [ ] A) HIV-1 RNA and proviral DNA
- [x] **B) Anti-HIV-1 (M and O) + anti-HIV-2 antibodies and p24 antigen** *(Correct Answer)*
- [ ] C) Only IgG antibodies
- [ ] D) CD4 count and viral load

> **Correct Answer:** **B) Anti-HIV-1 (M and O) + anti-HIV-2 antibodies and p24 antigen**
>
> **Explanation & Rationale:** A 4th-generation ELISA (combination/combo antigen-antibody test) simultaneously detects anti-HIV-1 (including M and O groups) and anti-HIV-2 antibodies as well as free HIV-1 p24 capsid antigen, narrowing the diagnostic window to ~14–18 days.
>
> **High-Yield Takeaway:** `4th-gen ELISA = HIV-1/2 antibodies + p24 antigen.`

### Q16. In the Fiebig stage, the earliest detectable biomarker after infection (Stage I, ~10–14 days) is:

- [ ] A) p24 antigen
- [ ] B) HIV antibodies by ELISA
- [x] **C) HIV RNA by nucleic acid testing** *(Correct Answer)*
- [ ] D) Western blot bands

> **Correct Answer:** **C) HIV RNA by nucleic acid testing**
>
> **Explanation & Rationale:** In the Fiebig staging of early HIV infection, Stage I (~10–14 days post-exposure) is defined by the appearance of viral RNA detectable by NAAT (nucleic acid amplification testing) before p24 antigen (Stage II) or antibodies (Stage III).
>
> **High-Yield Takeaway:** `Earliest Fiebig stage I marker: HIV RNA by NAAT (~10–14 days).`

> **High-Yield Takeaway:** `Viral load assay: Sensitivity 20–40 copies/mL; linearity 20 to 10^7 copies/mL.`

### Q19. According to the clinical case on late presentation, virological failure is defined as:

### Q20. In the newborn of an HIV-infected mother, a negative HIV DNA/RNA PCR at day 3 must be repeated after 6 weeks because:

- [ ] A) The infant PCR never becomes positive before 1 year
- [x] **B) Maternal IgG antibodies persist up to 18 months and can confuse serology** *(Correct Answer)*
- [ ] C) The antiviral prophylaxis interferes with PCR
- [ ] D) PCR is not recommended in newborns

> **Correct Answer:** **B) Maternal IgG antibodies persist up to 18 months and can confuse serology**
>
> **Explanation & Rationale:** Maternal IgG antibodies cross the placenta and can persist in the infant's circulation for up to 18 months, rendering antibody tests falsely positive. A negative PCR at birth/day 3 must be repeated at 6 weeks (and after cessation of breastfeeding) to detect intrapartum/early postpartum transmission.
>
> **High-Yield Takeaway:** `Maternal IgG persists up to 18 months; infant diagnosis requires PCR at 6 weeks.`

>
> **High-Yield Takeaway:** `Orthomyxoviridae contains 7 genera.`

### Q3. Which of the following is NOT a genus of the family Orthomyxoviridae?

### Q4. Influenza viruses are classified by their nucleoprotein and have which genome type?

- [ ] A) Positive-sense single-stranded RNA, non-segmented

> **Correct Answer:** **B) Negative-sense single-stranded RNA, segmented**
>
> **Explanation & Rationale:** Influenza viruses possess a negative-sense single-stranded RNA (-ssRNA) genome that is segmented (8 segments in A and B; 7 in C and D).
>
> **High-Yield Takeaway:** `Influenza genome: Segmented, negative-sense single-stranded RNA (-ssRNA).`

### Q5. Influenza A and B viruses each possess how many gene segments?

- [ ] B) 7
- [x] **C) 8** *(Correct Answer)*
- [ ] D) 10

> **Correct Answer:** **C) 8**
>
> **Explanation & Rationale:** Influenza A and B viruses each have 8 distinct negative-sense RNA gene segments encoding structural and non-structural proteins.
>
> **High-Yield Takeaway:** `Influenza A and B genomes = 8 RNA segments.`

### Q6. Reassortment — the exchange of viral RNA segments — has been reported:

- [x] **A) Within each genus or type, but not across types** *(Correct Answer)*
- [ ] B) Across all four influenza types freely
- [ ] C) Only between influenza A and B
- [ ] D) Only in influenza C

> **Correct Answer:** **A) Within each genus or type, but not across types**
>
> **Explanation & Rationale:** Reassortment (antigenic shift) occurs when two different viruses coinfect the same cell, exchanging RNA segments. This occurs within the same genus (e.g., between Influenza A strains) but NOT across different genera/types (A cannot reassort with B).
>
> **High-Yield Takeaway:** `Reassortment occurs within each genus/type, not across types.`

> **High-Yield Takeaway:** `Influenza A subtypes: 18 HA and 11 NA.`

>
> **High-Yield Takeaway:** `Influenza B lineages: Yamagata and Victoria.`

### Q9. The WHO standard nomenclature system for naming influenza viruses has been in use since:

### Q10. The infectious spherical form of influenza A and B virions measures:

- [ ] C) 200–300 nm in diameter
- [ ] D) 500 nm in diameter

>

>
> **High-Yield Takeaway:** `Influenza virion size: 80–120 nm in diameter.`

### Q11. Hemagglutinin on the viral surface functions primarily to:

- [ ] A) Destroy sialic acid receptors
- [x] **B) Bind to sialic acids on the respiratory epithelium (cell entry)** *(Correct Answer)*
- [ ] C) Bud new virions from the cell membrane
- [ ] D) Block interferon signaling

> **Correct Answer:** **B) Bind to sialic acids on the respiratory epithelium (cell entry)**
>
> **Explanation & Rationale:** Hemagglutinin (HA) is the viral attachment protein and major antigen. It binds to terminal sialic acid (N-acetylneuraminic acid) residues on host respiratory epithelial cell surface glycoproteins.
>
> **High-Yield Takeaway:** `HA function: Binds host cell surface sialic acids for viral entry.`

### Q12. Within hemagglutinin, which region is highly conserved between subtypes?

- [ ] A) HA1 globular head
- [x] **B) HA2 stalk region** *(Correct Answer)*
- [ ] C) The sialic acid binding pocket
- [ ] D) The transmembrane anchor only

> **Correct Answer:** **B) HA2 stalk region**
>
> **Explanation & Rationale:** While the globular head (HA1 domain) is highly variable due to antigenic drift, the HA2 stalk (stem) domain is highly conserved across subtypes and is the primary target for universal influenza vaccines.
>
> **High-Yield Takeaway:** `HA2 stalk (stem) region is highly conserved across influenza subtypes.`

### Q13. Neuraminidase is a sialidase that is required for which step of the viral life cycle?

- [ ] A) Attachment to the host cell
- [x] **B) Viral budding and release from the infected cell** *(Correct Answer)*
- [ ] C) Nuclear import of viral RNA
- [ ] D) Reverse transcription

> **Correct Answer:** **B) Viral budding and release from the infected cell**
>
> **Explanation & Rationale:** Neuraminidase (NA) is a receptor-destroying sialidase enzyme that cleaves terminal sialic acids from host cell surface receptors and budding virions, preventing self-aggregation and enabling virion release/spread.
>

### Q18. Which of the following is a common clinical manifestation of acute viral hepatitis, often observed as a yellowish discoloration of the skin and eyes?

> **Correct Answer:** **B) Jaundice**
>

### Q21. Which of the following is a crucial prevention strategy for Hepatitis A and E?

### Q22. What is a long-term complication associated with chronic Hepatitis B and C infections?

> **Correct Answer:** **C) Liver cirrhosis and cancer**
>
